# Strength Training, Hypertrophy, & Progressive Overload

## Principles of Progressive Overload
Muscular hypertrophy and strength adaptations are stimulated primarily by mechanical tension under controlled tempo. 
To sustain adaptations over time, athletes must apply progressive overload systematically across training blocks. 
Methods of progressive overload include:
1. Increasing external resistance (load in kilograms/pounds) while keeping repetitions constant.
2. Increasing total repetitions performed per set within target rep brackets.
3. Increasing total weekly working sets per muscle group (typically 10 to 20 hard sets weekly).
4. Improving execution quality, range of motion, and eccentric control (3-4 second negative phase).

## Repetition Ranges and Rest Intervals
For maximal strength development (myofibrillar hypertrophy), heavy compound movements (squat, deadlift, bench press, overhead press) are performed in the 1 to 5 repetition range at 80-90% 1RM with 3 to 5 minutes rest to replenish intramuscular phosphocreatine (PCr) stores. 
For general muscular hypertrophy (sarcoplasmic & myofibrillar balance), 6 to 12 repetitions per set at 65-80% 1RM with 90 to 180 seconds rest maximizes metabolic stress and mechanical recruitment of motor units. 
For muscular endurance, 15 to 25 repetitions with short 45-60 second rest periods induce high capillary density and intracellular buffer capacity.

## Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) and Reps in Reserve (RIR)
Training to absolute concentric failure on every set causes disproportionate central nervous system (CNS) fatigue without proportional hypertrophic benefit. 
Athletes should program working sets at RPE 7 to 9 (1 to 3 Reps in Reserve), saving true failure only for the final set of isolation exercises. 
Monitoring RIR prevents premature plateaus and protects connective tissue tendons and ligaments from overuse inflammation.
