# Recovery Protocols, Periodization & Injury Prevention

## Active Recovery and Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness (DOMS)
Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness (DOMS) results from micro-trauma to myofibrils and localized inflammatory cascades following strenuous eccentric resistance training. 
Total sedentary inactivity often exacerbates stiffness. Active recovery—such as 20 to 30 minutes of low-intensity walking, swimming, or light cycling at Zone 1 (50-60% HR max)—promotes systemic blood circulation, clears cellular metabolic debris, and accelerates nutrient delivery without generating additional mechanical fatigue.

## Dynamic Warmup vs Static Stretching
Pre-workout warmups should be strictly dynamic: incorporating joint mobilizations, leg swings, arm circles, lunges with thoracic rotation, and muscle activation drills (glute bridges, band pull-aparts) for 8 to 12 minutes. Dynamic movements elevate core muscle temperature and synovial fluid viscosity in joint capsules. 
Static stretching (holding positions for >30 seconds) should be reserved strictly for post-workout cool-downs or standalone mobility sessions, as pre-exercise static stretching can transiently reduce maximal muscular power output.

## Overtraining Syndrome and Deload Weeks
Prolonged high-intensity training without scheduled deloads leads to autonomic nervous system imbalance, chronic elevation of cortisol, suppressed immune function, and sleep fragmentation. 
If resting heart rate elevates by >8-10 bpm above baseline, or morning grip strength drops noticeably, the athlete is showing markers of overreaching. 
Deload weeks should be scheduled every 4 to 6 weeks, reducing total volume by 40-50% while maintaining moderate intensity to allow systemic fatigue dissipation.
